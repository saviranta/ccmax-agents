# Skill: Business Report

This skill defines how to write a business-oriented research report. Load this file when the report type is `business-report`.

---

## Purpose

Communicate research findings to decision-makers and non-technical stakeholders. The goal is to enable a clear, confident business decision. Every section should connect back to business impact, cost, or risk.

---

## Audience

- Executives, managers, founders
- Non-technical stakeholders
- People with limited time who need clear takeaways

Write as if the reader will skim headings first, then read sections of interest. Lead with conclusions, not process.

---

## Structure

### 1. Executive Summary
- 1–3 paragraphs, maximum half a page
- State the business question, the recommendation, and the primary reason for it
- Include the most critical number or risk if one exists
- Written last, placed first

### 2. Background and Context
- Why this question matters now
- Relevant constraints: budget, timeline, existing systems, regulations
- Scope: what is and is not covered in this report
- Keep brief — 1–2 paragraphs

### 3. Key Findings
- The most important things discovered during research
- Use bullet points or a short numbered list for scannability
- Each finding should be a standalone statement, not a sentence fragment
- Support findings with data, citations, or named sources where available
- 3–7 findings is typical; more than 10 suggests the section needs pruning

### 4. Options and Alternatives
- Present 2–4 concrete options (including the status quo if relevant)
- For each option, cover:
  - Brief description
  - Pros (business benefits, cost savings, strategic fit)
  - Cons (risks, costs, dependencies, downsides)
  - Estimated cost or effort if determinable
- A simple pros/cons table is acceptable here if it aids clarity

### 5. Recommendation
- State the recommended option directly in the first sentence
- Give 2–3 reasons tied to the findings and business priorities
- Acknowledge the strongest counterargument and address it briefly
- If the decision depends on conditions (e.g., budget approval, further data), say so explicitly

### 6. Next Steps
- A short, actionable list — typically 3–6 items
- Each step should have a clear owner role (e.g., "Engineering team to...") and a rough timeframe if possible
- Ordered by sequence, not importance

### 7. Appendix
- Supporting data, methodology notes, raw tables, or extended analysis that would clutter the main body
- Label each appendix section (Appendix A, Appendix B, etc.)
- Reference appendix sections from the relevant body sections

---

## Tone and Style

- Professional and direct — no filler phrases ("it is important to note that...")
- Use plain language; define any technical term on first use
- Active voice preferred
- Quantify wherever possible: percentages, dollar amounts, timeframes, user counts
- Avoid hedging without cause; if something is uncertain, say so and explain why

---

## Visuals

- Include a chart or table when quantitative data supports a finding or comparison
- Tables work well for options comparison and cost breakdowns
- Bar or line charts work well for trends over time
- Every visual must have a title and a caption explaining what to take from it
- Do not include visuals that do not add information beyond the surrounding text

---

## Length Guidance

- Main body (sections 1–6): 2–4 pages
- Appendix: as needed, no strict limit
- If the main body exceeds 4 pages, review for content that belongs in the appendix or can be cut

---

## Format Requirements

- Use heading levels consistently: `##` for main sections, `###` for subsections
- Use bullet points for lists of 3 or more items
- Use bold to highlight the single most important phrase in a section, sparingly
- Do not use footnotes; cite sources inline with author/organization and year, or as a numbered reference list at the end
