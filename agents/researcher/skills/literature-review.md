# Skill: Literature Review

This skill defines how to write an academic-style literature review. Load this file when the report type is `literature-review`.

---

## Purpose

Synthesize the existing body of knowledge on a topic, identify what is established, what is contested, and what is unknown. This is not a summary of individual papers — it is an analysis of what the collective evidence says. The output should help researchers and domain experts understand the state of knowledge and where productive inquiry remains.

---

## Audience

- Researchers and academics in the relevant field
- Domain experts and practitioners who follow the literature
- Graduate students or new entrants to a field seeking orientation
- Assumes comfort with academic language, citations, and evidence standards

---

## Structure

### 1. Executive Summary
- 1–2 paragraphs
- State the topic, the scope of the review, the main conclusion about the state of knowledge, and the most significant gap identified
- Accessible to informed non-specialists

### 2. Introduction and Scope
- Define the topic precisely: what is included and what is not
- State the central question or questions the review addresses
- Explain why a review is warranted now (volume of recent work, emerging consensus or controversy, practical importance)
- Identify the temporal scope (e.g., "literature from 2010 to present") and any geographic or linguistic scope limitations

### 3. Methodology
- Document the search strategy so the review is reproducible:
  - Databases searched (e.g., PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, ACM Digital Library)
  - Search terms and Boolean logic used
  - Date ranges applied
  - Filters applied (peer-reviewed only, language, study type)
- Inclusion and exclusion criteria: what types of sources were included (empirical studies, systematic reviews, grey literature) and why
- Source quality assessment criteria (see Required Elements below)
- Total number of sources identified, screened, and included

### 4. Thematic Analysis
- Organize findings by theme, not by source
- Each theme is a subsection: state what the body of literature says about this theme, where there is agreement, where there is disagreement, and what the methodological quality of the evidence is
- Do not write "Smith (2019) found X; Jones (2020) found Y; Lee (2021) found Z" — synthesize: "Evidence consistently supports X (Smith 2019, Jones 2020), though one study found Y under conditions Z (Lee 2021)"
- Typical themes per review: 3–6; more than 8 suggests the scope is too broad
- Within each theme, note the strength of evidence using the Evidence Strength Ratings defined below

### 5. Synthesis
- What does the combined body of evidence say in answer to the review's central question?
- Where does the evidence converge and where does it conflict?
- What methodological limitations run across the literature (e.g., small sample sizes, self-reported data, publication bias, lack of longitudinal studies)?
- This section moves beyond summarizing individual themes to making a judgment about the overall state of knowledge

### 6. Gaps in Knowledge
- What questions remain unanswered?
- What populations, contexts, or conditions are understudied?
- What methodological improvements would most strengthen future work?
- Where findings are contested, what would it take to resolve the disagreement?
- Be specific: "More research is needed" is not a gap; "No studies have examined X in population Y using longitudinal methods" is a gap

### 7. Conclusions
- Summarize the state of knowledge in 2–3 paragraphs
- State practical implications if any exist
- Note the limitations of this review itself (scope, search strategy, potential for missed sources)

### 8. References
- Full citation list in a consistent academic format (APA, APA 7th, or Vancouver — state which is used)
- Every source cited in the body must appear in the references; no orphan citations
- Include DOI or stable URL where available

---

## Tone and Style

- Rigorous and precise: use exact language, avoid vague quantifiers ("some studies" → "three of the seven studies reviewed")
- Neutral: do not advocate for a position; represent the evidence fairly including findings that contradict a preferred conclusion
- Evidence-based: every claim about the state of knowledge must be traceable to cited sources
- Hedge appropriately: distinguish between "the evidence strongly suggests", "preliminary evidence indicates", and "one study found"
- Passive voice is acceptable when the agent is not important; active voice is preferred when it aids clarity

---

## Required Elements

### Source Quality Assessment
Every review must assess the quality of its sources. Apply a consistent framework and state which framework is used. Acceptable frameworks:

- **For empirical research**: note study design hierarchy (RCT > cohort > case-control > cross-sectional > case study), sample size, and whether the study was peer-reviewed
- **For systematic reviews and meta-analyses**: note the PRISMA compliance or equivalent quality indicator if available
- **For grey literature or practitioner reports**: note the organization, methodology transparency, and potential conflicts of interest

Present source quality as either a summary paragraph in the Methodology section or a table in the Appendix.

### Evidence Strength Ratings
Assign each major finding in the Thematic Analysis a strength rating. Use one of these tiers and apply it consistently:

- **Strong**: multiple independent high-quality studies with consistent findings
- **Moderate**: some quality studies with generally consistent findings, or one high-quality study
- **Preliminary**: limited studies, small samples, or mixed findings
- **Contested**: studies exist on both sides with no clear resolution; note the nature of the disagreement

State the rating in brackets after the synthesized finding, e.g.: "Evidence strongly supports a relationship between X and Y [Strong]."

---

## Length Guidance

- Main body: 5–10 pages
- Thematic Analysis is typically the longest section
- References section is not counted toward page length
- If the review exceeds 10 pages, consider whether the scope is too broad or whether themes can be consolidated

---

## Format Requirements

- Use `##` for main sections, `###` for themes within the Thematic Analysis
- Use a consistent citation format throughout; state it in the Methodology section
- A source table (author, year, study type, quality rating, theme) in the Appendix is strongly recommended for reviews covering more than 15 sources
- Do not use footnotes for substantive content; incorporate into the body or cut
- Block quotes from sources are acceptable when the exact wording is analytically significant; limit to 2–3 per review
